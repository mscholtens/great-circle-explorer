# ACCURACY — assumptions, measured accuracy and known limitations

**Great Circle Explorer is a project for learning and exploration only. It is not for production use, and not for real-life navigation, flight or voyage planning, surveying, legal or civil-time decisions, or anything where a wrong number could cause harm.** The formulas are hand-written to be understood, on a deliberately simple model of the Earth, with data that is simplified, dated and not authoritative. Use official charts, instruments and services for anything real.

This page lists every simplification the app makes, and how far off the results are, measured against independent references. The numbers come from [`tools/verify_accuracy.py`](../tools/verify_accuracy.py) (fixed random seed, repeatable; it needs `geographiclib`, `ephem` and `timezonefinder` in a separate environment, they are not dependencies of the app). The test suite stores a set of reference values from the same libraries, so regressions are caught without them.

## 2. What the spherical model means

- **Distances** are measured along a sphere of mean radius 6371.0088 km. The real Earth is an ellipsoid (WGS84): 6378.137 km at the equator, 6356.752 km at the poles. The spherical distance is therefore up to about 0.5 % too long or too short, depending on latitude and direction. The *Ellipsoid distance, Vincenty* card shows the better value next to it (and Lambert's formula as an approximation).
- **Latitudes are used as if they were latitudes on a sphere.** GPS and airport coordinates are *geodetic* latitudes on the WGS84 ellipsoid; the app does not convert them (the difference is up to 0.19°, and the cause of the bearing differences above).
- **Bearings and courses** are true azimuths from true north, on the sphere. Magnetic variation is not applied. They are the direction of the great circle *at the given coordinates*; runway headings, departure and arrival procedures are not known.
- **Heights are ignored.** Distances are at sea level. An aircraft at 11 km altitude flies a path about 0.17 % longer than the surface distance; mountains are not modelled.
- **Very short and very long routes.** Identical points have no bearings. Antipodal points have infinitely many great circles, so bearings, midpoint and path are undefined (the distance is still πR). Within 10⁻⁶ rad of antipodal the path is not drawn (numerically unstable). At a pole every direction is south or north, so bearings from a pole are undefined, and the rhumb line is undefined.

## 3. Assumptions of individual cards

- **Distance analysis:** the ten rows describe the same two points but differ for two different reasons, which is the point of the card: the **method** (a path over a surface, on a flat map, or through the Earth) and the **model of the Earth** (a sphere of some radius, or the ellipsoid). Every row is tagged `sphere` or `ellipsoid`. The reference is Vincenty's distance on the WGS84 ellipsoid (agreement with Karney's algorithm to 0.1 mm); Lambert's when Vincenty does not converge (nearly antipodal pairs, with reduced accuracy and a warning); the sphere when neither exists. **The conventional method is the great circle on the mean-radius sphere** (R = 6371.0088 km, the value used by most tools): against the reference it is typically within 0.2 % and at most about 0.5 % (a meridian arc across the equator is about +0.56 %); on Cologne → Santiago it is +0.17 %. The three great-circle rows (polar, mean, equatorial radius) use the same formula and angle, so the choice of sphere alone moves the answer by up to about 0.34 % (12 034 to 12 074 km). *Degrees as a flat grid* (111.195 km per degree both ways) and the *programmer's shortcut* (equirectangular: longitude scaled by cos of the mean latitude) are deliberately naive: they can be too long or too short, and the shortcut is only good for short distances. The *flat Mercator ruler* uses the map scale at the equator and overstates distances away from it; the *rhumb line* is longer than the great circle by an amount that depends on direction and latitude. The sphere-based rows are compared with the reference, so their difference includes the sphere's own model error; they therefore also show their difference to the great circle on the same sphere. The *straight tunnel* is computed on the ellipsoid (Earth-centred coordinates) and is not a travel distance. None of these is a flight distance: real flights follow airways and are typically 5–10 % longer than the great circle. The *how far apart are the paths* number is measured from 48 points of the great circle to 720 points of the rhumb line (accurate to about 10 km).

- **Flight time:** distance ÷ a constant cruise speed (default 850 km/h), plus 15 min for climb and descent (airborne time), plus 15 min for taxiing (gate to gate). Both allowances are rough assumptions. No wind (the jet stream can change a real flight by an hour or more), no airways (real routes are typically 5–10 % longer than the great circle), no holding, no runways. The *elapsed time at a crossing* is the share of the route times the airborne time (constant speed).
- **Light and fiber latency:** a cable that follows the great circle exactly, refractive index 1.468 for single-mode fiber, no routers or switches. Real round-trip times are typically 1.5–3× higher.
- **Time at reference speeds:** constant speed in a straight line along the surface; the ISS speed is its orbital speed, used only as a comparison.
- **Gravity train:** a uniform-density Earth, no friction, no air, no Earth rotation. The real Earth is denser in the core, which would shorten the trip (about 38 minutes through the centre instead of 42.2).
- **Horizon and line of sight:** a smooth sphere, no refraction (which extends the real horizon by roughly 8 %), no terrain or buildings; the maximum distance two observers can see each other is the sum of their two horizons.
- **Mercator distance:** a straight ruler line on a flat Mercator map, with longitudes taken the short way around; it overstates distances away from the equator by design. Only defined up to 85.0511° latitude.
- **Tropics and polar circles:** at ±23.44° and ±66.56°, a fixed approximation of the Earth's axial tilt.
- **Solar time offset:** 15° of longitude = 1 hour; it is the position of the Sun, not civil time.

## 4. The Sun and the Moon

- The Sun's position uses low-precision formulas (mean longitude, mean anomaly, equation of centre, obliquity). They are meant for about 1950–2050, which is why the app's date range is 1970–2049. Nutation, aberration details and the small terms of the planets are left out; that is what the 0.005° and 0.07 min above come from.
- **Sunrise and sunset** use the Sun's centre at −0.833° altitude (standard refraction 34′ plus the solar semi-diameter 16′). Real refraction varies with temperature, pressure and the exact horizon; the observer's height and the surrounding terrain are ignored. Times are UTC clock times and may fall on the previous or next UTC date (marked on the card). The chosen date is the local solar day at the place.
- **Near the midnight sun** (days longer than about 21 h, close to the polar circles) the Sun skims the horizon, so a tiny difference in its position becomes a larger difference in the sunrise time: up to about 10 minutes, rarely 15, against 1 minute or less for days up to 8 hours long (winter days at 70°N are accurate). The first or last day of the midnight sun or polar night can be off by a day. The card says so for days longer than 21 h.
- **Moon:** a short series of sine terms for the Moon's longitude and latitude (about 0.3°), combined with the Sun to give the elongation. The "percent lit" ignores the small effect of the Moon's distance. Eclipses and libration are not modelled. Everyone on Earth sees the same phase on a given day, so the card does not depend on A or B.

## 5. Time zones

- **The rules are today's.** UTC offsets and daylight-saving rules come from the IANA tz database release shown on the card (2026f), bundled with the app so results do not depend on the operating system. Future political decisions (new zones, changes to daylight saving) cannot be predicted, so dates after today assume the current rules simply continue. The app accepts dates from 1 January 1970 to 31 December 2049.
- **Past dates use today's borders** with each zone's historical offsets: a region that changed zone since then is shown with the history of its present zone.
- **Borders are simplified** (Douglas-Peucker, about 3 km) from timezone-boundary-builder to keep the file small, so a point within a few kilometres of a border may resolve to the neighbouring zone. Overlapping polygons are resolved in favour of the smallest (most specific) one; a point within 15 km of a polygon but in none (coast or simplification gap) gets the nearest zone, and the card says so; further out at sea the nominal zone `round(longitude / 15°)` is used.
- **The offset is evaluated at 12:00 UTC** of the chosen date; around a daylight-saving switch the difference between two places can be different earlier or later that day.
- Disputed regions and unofficial zones follow the tz database (for example `Asia/Urumqi`, UTC+6, for Xinjiang, although the official time there is Beijing time).

## 6. Bundled data

- **Map:** Natural Earth 1:50m country outlines, simplified and rounded. Borders and names are not authoritative, and some are disputed.
- **Airports and cities:** OurAirports (large and medium airports with an IATA code and scheduled service) and Natural Earth populated places (cities of 300 000+). The data is a snapshot and can contain errors; airport coordinates are the airport reference points, not a runway. A city of fewer than 300 000 people (for example Groningen) is not listed, although its airport may be.

## 7. How this was checked, and what is left

Independent references used by `tools/verify_accuracy.py`: **GeographicLib** (exact geodesics on a sphere and on WGS84, Karney's algorithm; the check `vincenty` compares our Vincenty implementation with it), **PyEphem** (positions of the Sun and the Moon, sunrise, new and full moon), **TimezoneFinder** (exact zone polygons). The unit tests additionally check analytic cases (quarter circles along the equator and a meridian, antipodes, the equinox, polar day and night), property tests over random pairs (triangle relations, the direct problem inverting the inverse problem, crossings versus brute force) and stored reference values.

Known and accepted limits: the spherical model (section 2); the simplified models of the travel cards (section 3); fixed refraction and the polar edge cases of sunrise (section 4); today's time-zone rules and simplified borders (section 5); snapshot data (section 6). If you find a number that disagrees with a trustworthy reference by more than is described here, that is a bug worth reporting.
